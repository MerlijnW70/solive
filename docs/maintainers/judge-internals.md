# How the judge works

The details behind [Judge work](../judge.md): the sandbox, what `judge` checks and in which order, and who is paid.

## Sandbox

Delivered work is untrusted, and a check that imports, builds or runs it runs the worker's code. So `judge` runs the command you pass with `--run` in a Docker container, `--sandbox docker`, the default. `judge` first makes a fresh temporary directory that holds a copy of only the files `judge` hashed, in `work`, a copy of the spec bytes it hashed, in `spec`, and a copy of the file or directory you pass with `--check`, in `check`; never the work or spec you passed. Each copied file keeps the executable bits of the original and no other mode; for the container every copy is also made readable to all. The container:

- has no network (`--network none`), a read-only root filesystem, no Linux capabilities and no way to gain privileges, keeps Docker's default seccomp filter, and runs as user 65534, not as you;
- has its own IPC and cgroup namespaces, no Docker log of its output, at most 1024 open files and no core dumps;
- has a capped number of processes, memory (`--sandbox-memory` such as `512m` or `2g`) and CPUs (`--sandbox-cpus` such as `1.5`), with the defaults in `packages/cli/src/sandbox.ts`; it writes only to an in-memory `/tmp` of `--sandbox-tmp` (default `256m`) that vanishes with it, and `HOME` is `/tmp`;
- sees the three copies read-only at `/judge/work`, `/judge/spec` and `/judge/check`, and nothing else from your machine: not your home directory, not your keypair, not the Docker socket;
- runs Docker's init (`--init`) as its first process, which reaps every process the check leaves behind, so no zombie holds a slot of the process cap;
- runs `--run` through `/bin/sh` in `/judge`, so `--check check.mjs --run "node check/check.mjs"` runs your check; a path on your machine does not exist in it;
- starts from an image pinned by its digest, by default `node:22-bookworm-slim@sha256:c3de60bf2f9dd0ac6370e6117950ff62d6e339527e7472301c9c78a017978392`, which holds Node.js 22. `--sandbox-image <name>@sha256:<digest>` picks another image, and `judge` refuses a reference without a digest.

`judge` pulls the image when Docker does not hold it yet, giving the pull `DOCKER_PULL_SECONDS` and each other question to Docker `DOCKER_ASK_SECONDS`, both in `packages/cli/src/sandbox.ts`. On timeout or Ctrl-C it stops the container with `docker kill` and removes it, with its anonymous volumes, by `docker rm -f -v`, and it removes the container after every run. The verdict records `sandbox: "docker"` and the `image`.

A container is not a virtual machine. The check shares your kernel, so a kernel or Docker exploit escapes the container; such exploits are out of scope here. Run `judge` on a machine that holds no other keys if that risk matters to you.

`--sandbox none` runs the check without a container, through your shell, as your user, with your files, your keys and your network: work the check runs can read your keypair and send it away, and it can steer the verdict you sign. `judge` refuses it unless you also pass `--i-trust-this-work`. Pass both only for work you wrote or fully trust. `judge` then warns on stderr that the check ran with your own rights, and the verdict records `sandbox: "none"` and, in `warnings`, that no isolation was used. The check then runs in the temporary directory, its working directory, with an environment that holds only `PATH`, `HOME`, `USERPROFILE`, `SYSTEMROOT`, `COMSPEC`, `PATHEXT`, `TEMP`, `TMP`, `TMPDIR`, `LANG` and `TERM` from yours, so your RPC URL, keypair path and other secrets do not reach it.

## Keys and output

`judge` reads the judge keypair and any `--encryption-keypair` in its own process, before the run, and signs only after the check has ended, in that process. None of them enters the container: the container sees the two copies, an environment of `HOME=/tmp`, `SPEC_PATH`, `ARTIFACT_PATH` and `ARTIFACT_SHA256` beside the image's own variables, and nothing else. The Docker client `judge` starts gets only `PATH`, `HOME` and the variables that name a Docker host.

The judged command is hostile. `judge` reads its output and shows at most 1000000 characters of it, with every control and invisible character written as `\uXXXX`, so no terminal escape or hidden text reaches a person or an agent. Nothing it writes is read back: the outcome depends only on its exit code and on the container's state as Docker reports it. The escrow, owner, nonce, result and payee come from the command line and the chain, never from the check.

## Outcomes

Every judgement that runs a check ends in one of three outcomes, decided by one classifier, `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts`, for every route: `judge` with each sandbox, with and without `--plain-node`, `judge --watch`, a committee member's `judge`, and `solive_wallet_judge_work`. A `fail` means the work was shown to fall short of the spec; `infra` means the cause cannot be put on the work; a `pass` means every check ran and passed. Only a pass is signed.

A fail exits 1 and writes the verdict. Infra signs nothing, writes no verdict, prints the reason on stderr and exits 75, so a caller runs the judge again. A refusal sends nothing and writes no verdict, and `judge` exits 78, `EXIT_REFUSED` in `packages/cli/src/judge-errors.ts`. `exitOfError` in `packages/cli/src/main.ts` maps every error of a `judge` run to its exit code: a failure `rpcTemporary` in `packages/cli/src/rpc-failure.ts` calls temporary, and a spec url unreadable for now, exit 75; a file named on the command line that is missing or cannot be read, or a `--verdict` that cannot be written or is neither a regular file nor a link, exits 2. Once the release was handed to the RPC node, a send that is not confirmed, or a verdict that cannot be written after it landed, is `ReleaseUncertain`, which names the intent to read and exits 76, `EXIT_SENT`. Other commands keep exit 1 for a refusal.

A refusal stays a refusal unless nothing but the work can cause it and every engine would judge the work the same. A delivered module that is not UTF-8 text stays a refusal, `NOT_TEXT` in `packages/cli/src/viper.ts`: Node's module loader decodes source as UTF-8 that replaces bad bytes, so plain Node can run such a module while ViperJS and the host engine cannot, and a fail would write a fail receipt that lets the payer reject a worker whose editor saved Windows-1252. A non-regular file in a directory `--artifact` stays a refusal: the judge reads it before any announcement binds it, the manifest hashes only regular files, and only the judge's own copy can hold one.

`judge` reads a container's end from Docker with `docker container inspect` before it removes the container, never from the exit code of the `docker run` client or from what the container prints, so a check that exits 125 or writes Docker's own error text is judged by its own exit code. The same goes for `docker run` failing before the container exists: inspect then finds no container, which is infra. A container that exits as the deadline is reached counts as stopped by the judge, so infra. A check that fills its memory without reaching `--sandbox-memory` exits by its own code; one that reaches it is killed by the kernel, which is infra. Give a check its own time limit per case, or pin `timeout` in a `judge-run` block, when it must fail work that hangs: `--timeout` is the judge's deadline, and reaching it says nothing about the work.

Docker sets `State.OOMKilled` from an event its runtime sends after the kernel counts the kill, and that event can be lost, for example when a check fills its memory within moments of starting: on the GitHub `ubuntu-24.04` runner the doctor's memory bomb ended with no flag while a slower bomb in the same job was flagged. A missing flag therefore proves nothing, and exit code 137 is all that is left, which a check can also give by killing itself with signal 9. So an exit code 137 under the memory the judge chose is infra, a self-kill included: the work gains nothing by it, since filling that memory gives the same infra. Under memory a `judge-run` block pins, a memory kill and a self-kill both fail, so 137 fails there: the check runs the work in its own processes, as the same user, so work that could turn 137 into infra could turn every fail into infra. The kernel's count, and with it the flag, rises for a kill by any out-of-memory killer, the host's global one included, so under memory a `judge-run` block pins a kill by the host's global killer also fails the run: once the container ended, `judge` can no longer read the container's `oom` count, which rises only at its own limit, so this residual stays on the Docker route, as the outcome table says. A 137 without the flag comes from a self-kill, a user-space killer such as `systemd-oomd` or `earlyoom`, `docker kill`, the Docker daemon or an operator. The flag, when set, is trusted: only the kernel's own count sets it, and a check reaches it only by using the memory.

The flag says that the kernel killed some process of the container for memory, not which one. `containerOutcome` in `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts` reads only Docker's `State`: `OOMKilled`, `ExitCode`, `Error` and whether it started, beside the judge's own deadline, the pinned `timeout` and `memory`, and its own clock. Under the memory the judge chose, the flag makes every exit code but 0 infra: the judge chose that memory, and a check that exits non-zero after a process of it was killed for it may have failed only because of that kill. A check that exits 0 still passes, as a plain Node case that answers rightly after a process it started was killed. This gives work no way out of a fail under memory a block pins: there the flag fails the run whatever the exit code, and the plain Node route fails a case when both of its counts rise.

A ViperJS case verdict `engine` comes from a fault the engine reports in its own code, one its types say cannot happen, or from a value it read after its own collector freed it: both are faults of the judge's engine, which a delivery cannot cause, so they are infra. A plain Node case that cannot even be started inside the container is infra the same way, as are a launcher that does not start, a delivery that cannot be staged and the deadline. Only the case process can write into the plain Node engine's own channel out of turn, so a case that does fails as `crashed`, the cases after it are not run, and the outcome is a fail. The `stopped` verdict of a case is a budget the spec pins or the judge fixes for every case, measured as CPU time plus the time the case waited by its own choice, so it stays a fail; a wall-clock backstop that stops a case before it used that budget is the judge's machine, so it is infra. `caseTiming` in `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts` decides both for every engine. A module whose top-level code waits on something that can never settle, so that the engine has nothing left to run, did not load: the host engine reports `no-module` for every case and plain Node fails the case as `crashed`, a fail. Top-level code that keeps running until the deadline stays infra.

The host engine notices that moment through Node's `beforeExit` event, which every supported Node raises once nothing is left to run. It does not use the `exit` event: on Node 22.12.0 a process whose entry module still awaits exits with code 13 whatever an `exit` listener sets, which made the same module infra there and a fail on later Node. `bash scripts/node-floor.sh` runs the judge test files on the Node version the `node-floor` CI job pins, downloaded once into your cache, without changing the Node you use.

These stop `judge` before any check runs, also as infra with exit 75: Docker is not installed, not running or does not answer in time; the image cannot be pulled, or its pull runs past its time limit. `judge --watch` prints an infra outcome as `verdict: "retry"` with `outcome: "infra"` and `retry: true` and tries the work again, and once one announcement reaches its limit of infra outcomes, `INFRA_RUNS` in `packages/cli/src/judge-watch.ts`, prints `verdict: "skipped"`. `solive_wallet_judge_work` returns `verdict: "infra"` with its `reason` and a `next` that approves nothing. A refusal that can change, such as another wallet's claim, prints `outcome: "deferred"` instead, never `infra`. `solive_wallet_judge_work` names a timeout by its own budget, `JUDGE_SECONDS` in `packages/cli/src/agent/wallet/judge-work.ts`, and a refusal comes back as a refused call with its code, never as a verdict.

## Pinned checks

A spec's `judge-run` block, described in [Judge work](../judge.md#pin-the-check-in-the-spec), pins the check. `pinnedRunOf` in `packages/cli/src/judge-run.ts` reads it with `readRunBlock` in `packages/client/src/run-block.ts`, which `publishSpec` in the SDK runs too, and with the same block reader as `judge-cases`, `packages/client/src/spec-blocks.ts`, which refuses either kind when a fence names it anywhere but on a plain top-level line, such as behind `>` or a list marker, indented, after a byte-order mark inside the text, or inside another fenced block, since a Markdown reader may see a block there that `judge` would not; a byte-order mark that opens the file is dropped, as Markdown readers drop it, and `solive_get_spec`, `intent-engine spec`, the hirer's checks and `judge` all call it on the same bytes. `runPlan` and `checkFilesOf` in `packages/cli/src/run-plan.ts` turn the spec and the judge's options into the check to run; `judge` calls them for every route, and `judge --watch` calls them once more before it watches, so a refused option stops the watch before it fetches anything.

The pinned `timeout` counts the container's setup. `watchPin` in `packages/cli/src/container-check.ts` takes `ranAtMostMs` from the start of the last status poll that saw the container `created`, and a run only passes or fails by its exit code when `ranAtMostMs` is below the pinned `timeout`; otherwise it is infra with `RUN_UNTIMED`. A spec pins `timeout` with headroom over the check's own time.

The options it handles: `--sandbox`, `--plain-node` and `--i-trust-this-work` allow only Docker; `--run` must equal `run` byte for byte; `--sandbox-image` must equal `image`; `--sandbox-memory` and `--sandbox-tmp` must name the same size, and `--sandbox-cpus` the same number, as the block or, for what it leaves out, the default; `--sandbox-gpu` is refused; `--check` follows `check_sha256`; `--timeout` must be at least `timeout` plus `RUN_OVERHEAD_SECONDS`. The other options pick the escrow, the work, the keys and where to write, not what runs.

`RUN_OVERHEAD_SECONDS` equals `DOCKER_ASK_SECONDS`, the time `judge` already gives Docker to answer one request, since creating and starting the container is one. Under a block `judge` times the check on its own monotonic clock, never with Docker's `StartedAt` and `FinishedAt`, which come from the Docker daemon's clock and can jump, so they may count a run shorter than it was. It asks Docker for the container's status every `STATUS_POLL_MS` from `packages/cli/src/container-check.ts`; once it sees the container running, it asks again the pinned `timeout` later, and a container still running then has run at least that long, so it is stopped and fails. The latest moment it saw the container still waiting to start bounds the start, so a container that ended less than the pinned `timeout` after it is judged by its exit code. Any other ended container is infra, as is one stopped at `--timeout` before it was seen running that long; a pinned-memory kill fails whatever the clock says.

`check_sha256` is `manifestSha256`, the encoding of a work directory, over the check files as they are mounted: each by its path under `/judge/check`, which starts with the name of the file or directory `--check` names, skipping `.git`, `node_modules` and `target`. A symbolic link or other special entry is refused. The judge mounts the bytes it hashed, every file executable, so the hash fixes what the check sees. The verdict records the block as `judgeRun`, beside `command` and `image`.

## Check a machine

`intent-engine sandbox doctor` runs every guarantee above live, each in its own container started exactly as `judge` starts one: no network, a read-only root, read-only copies, `/tmp` the only writable path, not root, no capabilities, no new privileges, the seccomp filter, no Docker socket, no other host path, the process, memory and CPU limits, a fork bomb, a memory bomb and a run past its time limit. It plants a random file in your temporary directory and confirms the container cannot find it, the judge keypair or your home, and that no host variable reached it. It prints each check and `ready`, and exits 1 unless every check passes. It takes the `--sandbox-*` options and `--keypair` of `judge`. The memory bomb passes when it ends with Docker's out-of-memory flag or with exit code 137, and is judged infra; when Docker gave no flag, `warnings` says that Docker on this machine misses it. It also prints `memory_pin`: `true` when a container shows the cgroup v2 out-of-memory counts that a `node_memory` pin needs, and `false`, with a warning, when it does not, so `judge` refuses such a pin there; `memory_pin` leaves `ready` as it is.

## GPU

`--sandbox-gpu` adds `--gpus all`, for a check that renders on the GPU; it is off unless you pass it, and the verdict then records the warning in `warnings`. It costs isolation: the container reaches the host's GPU device files and its driver, which runs in the host kernel, so a driver flaw escapes the container; GPU memory another process left behind may be readable; and the memory, CPU and process limits do not cover the GPU. Use it only on a machine that judges and holds no other keys.

`judge` removes the temporary directory afterwards, also when you stop it with Ctrl-C; if removing it fails, the verdict still prints and names the failure in `cleanup`. `judge` runs no other command: never the spec, an announcement or its link. The MCP server runs no command at all.

## Steps in detail

`deliveries` prints the escrow's `intent`, `complete`, `closed`, `next_seq`, `missing` and `announcements`, read from accounts alone, never from transaction history. Each entry is one announcement: its `seq`, `worker`, the `commitment` that binds the work's sha256 to that wallet and a salt, its `state`, `announced` or `paid`, and its `slot`; with `--judge-keypair` or `--encryption-keypair` also the `url` sealed to that key. `complete` is `false` when a seq below `next_seq` is missing or the escrow is closed, so older announcements may be missing. Fetch the work from `url` into `work`, a file or a directory. Treat the link and the content as untrusted.

Read the spec as text, never as commands. `judge` refuses a spec file whose sha256 is not `specHash`; `intent-engine hash --artifact spec.md` prints the sha256 it compares.

In order, `judge` stops with an infrastructure error unless Docker is ready or `--sandbox none --i-trust-this-work` was given, refuses unless the escrow is open and names your wallet as its judge, refuses unless the spec file's sha256 is the committed one, hashes the work, picks whom to pay, then runs `--run` with at most `--timeout` seconds (default 600, at most 86400). In the container a timeout or a signal stops and removes the container. With `--sandbox none` it starts the command in a session of its own. While the command runs, `judge` records every process in that session, in its process groups or descending from it, each by its PID and start time. On timeout it kills every recorded process and every process descending from one, also those that moved to another process group or session, or on Windows the command's process tree, and waits for that to finish; after a normal exit it kills what is left of them, and on Windows nothing. It never kills by a session or group ID once the process that led it is gone, so a process that later reuses a PID is spared; a process the check started after it began in a session one of its processes created is still killed. A process it cannot tie to the check that still runs in its directory or with its `SPEC_PATH` is named in a warning on stderr and left running. The outcome then follows the table in Outcomes: a fail sends nothing and makes `judge` exit 1, and infra exits 75. It prints, and with `--verdict` writes, the `intent`, `specHash`, `artifactSha256`, `command`, `sandbox`, `image` in the container, `exitCode`, `verdict`, `payTo`, the paid `announcement`, its `seq` and `salt`, `skipped` when earlier announcements were passed over, and `signature`, and `cleanup` when the copy could not be removed. Before any check or refusal it deletes an existing `--verdict` file, and writes it only once a verdict is reached. A SIGINT or SIGTERM during the check kills it and is never a pass: `judge` sends nothing, writes no verdict, removes its copy and exits 130 or 143. A signal while it removes the copy lets the removal finish first. Ctrl-C still stops the payment after a pass until the release is signed and handed to the RPC node: a SIGINT or SIGTERM before that sends nothing, writes no verdict and exits 130 or 143. A signal once the release is handed over waits for the send, then names its signature, or says the release may have landed when the send failed, and exits 130 or 143. A second signal then exits at once, says the release may have landed, names its signature and writes no verdict. Once the release is confirmed, a signal no longer stops anything: `judge` writes the verdict, names the signature and then exits 130 or 143.

On a pass this sends nothing yet. When the escrow names your wallet, not a committee, `judge` simulates the release it would send and adds `simulated` to the verdict: `{ "ok": true, "units_consumed": <n> }`, or `{ "ok": false, "error": <e> }` with the engine error's name, such as `FillPaused` or `ClaimHeld`, or else the reason the release cannot be built, so a release that would fail is known before `--yes`. `verdict` still reports the check alone. Add `--yes` to run the check again and settle:

The release pays `payTo` with the result `--result` names, by default the escrow's least result, through the announcement the verdict names, which the program marks paid together with the work's sha256 and salt. `judge` refuses before running the check when `--result` lies outside the results the escrow accepts. The payer reads the release with `intent-engine receipt --owner <owner> --nonce <nonce> --sha256 <hash of the work received>`, or with `solive_wallet_receipt`. Add `--release <signature>`, or `release`, with the `signature` the judge printed: the receipt then reads that one transaction and no history, so zero-lamport transfers sent to the escrow cannot push the release out of reach. Without it the receipt searches the escrow's history and says `complete: false` when it could not read all of it. The receipt names the paid `announcement` and its `announcer`, the earlier announcements it passed over that are still on chain, and every refutation of it with its standing.

## The ViperJS engine

`--sandbox viper` judges a delivery that is one JavaScript module whose default export is a function, with no Docker and no check of yours. It runs the module in [ViperJS](https://github.com/MerlijnW70/viperjs), a JavaScript engine compiled to a WebAssembly module that imports nothing: the delivery has no files, network, clock or host to reach. The cases come from the spec, so the escrow's spec hash pins them. The spec holds exactly one block fenced as `judge-cases`, a JSON object with a `cases` list of `{"args": [...], "expected": ...}`, where every argument and expected answer is a JSON string, number, boolean or null; the word count spec ends with one. Every engine receives each argument exactly as the spec writes it, -0 included. ViperJS takes strings as UTF-8, which cannot carry a lone surrogate such as `"\ud800"`, so `judge` refuses, when it reads the spec, a spec whose arguments, expected answers, draw generator or draw checker hold one, naming the case, as it refuses an expected -0; drawn arguments that hold one refuse the spec the same way, before the delivery runs. Nothing is run or paid. Optional `steps` (default 10000000) and `heap` in bytes (default 67108864) set the budgets, and optional `node_memory` pins the memory of the plain Node engine below. Optional `seed` and `clock` pin the seed of `Math.random` and the time `Date` reads; left out, `judge` draws both at random for each judgement and records them in the verdict's `cases`, so a delivery cannot recognise the judge by them and anyone can rerun the judgement with the same values. The seed drives every engine: the host engine and the plain Node engine below start Node with V8's `--random-seed` set to 1 plus the seed modulo 2147483647, since V8 keeps only the low bits of a larger number and reads 0 as no seed at all, so `Math.random` in them is reproducible from the verdict too. Each plain Node case runs in a fresh process, so its draws start again from the seed in every case, while the host engine's draws run on across its cases. The clock is pinned in ViperJS only; the host and plain Node engines read the real time.

Each case runs in a fresh engine and the answer is compared outside JavaScript, so a delivery that rewrites built-ins cannot pass, and an object, even one that converts to the answer, fails. An answer of `undefined` fails, in published and drawn cases alike: ViperJS reports it as `wrong` with no `detail`, the host and plain Node engines as `not-primitive`. The checker of a draw sees only answers the published-case rules accept, which `answerFault` in `packages/cli/src/draw.ts` names for any engine: null, a boolean, a finite number, or a string with no lone surrogate. A string with a lone surrogate, and a `NaN`, `Infinity` or `-Infinity`, fails its case as `not-primitive` with a `detail` that names the reason, after the confirmation run of long answers and before the checker runs; U+FFFD is an answer like any other.

ViperJS compares strings unit for unit and carries string values both ways as WTF-8: a lone surrogate is the three bytes from ED A0 80 to ED BF BF, a surrogate pair the four bytes of its character. `packages/cli/src/wtf8.ts` encodes the arguments and expected answers it sends and decodes the answers it reads exactly, so `"\ud800"` arrives as that code unit and `"�"` as U+FFFD. Bytes that are neither UTF-8 nor a surrogate triple, or a pair written as two triples, refuse the output as broken with the reason named; nothing is replaced. The `threw` and `no-module` messages stay UTF-8, as ViperJS writes them. The same spec and delivery give the same verdict on any machine. `judge` passes only when every case passes, and the verdict lists `cases` with the failures and `engine` with the sha256 of the module and the ViperJS commit it was built from. `judge` refuses a module whose sha256 differs from the one it pins; `scripts/viper-judge.sh` in this repository rebuilds it and checks that hash.

A delivery can tell that it runs in ViperJS, which has no `Intl` or `WebAssembly`, and answer correctly only there. So `judge` runs every case a second time in another engine, the host engine: a fresh V8 context in a separate Node process with the JavaScript built-ins, `Intl` included, but no `require`, `eval` or imports, no environment, and no file access. Next to plain Node, the host engine lacks exactly these globals: `AbortController`, `AbortSignal`, `Blob`, `BroadcastChannel`, `Buffer`, `ByteLengthQueuingStrategy`, `CompressionStream`, `CountQueuingStrategy`, `Crypto`, `CryptoKey`, `CustomEvent`, `DOMException`, `DecompressionStream`, `Event`, `EventTarget`, `File`, `FormData`, `Headers`, `MessageChannel`, `MessageEvent`, `MessagePort`, `Navigator`, `Performance`, `PerformanceEntry`, `PerformanceMark`, `PerformanceMeasure`, `PerformanceObserver`, `PerformanceObserverEntryList`, `PerformanceResourceTiming`, `ReadableByteStreamController`, `ReadableStream`, `ReadableStreamBYOBReader`, `ReadableStreamBYOBRequest`, `ReadableStreamDefaultController`, `ReadableStreamDefaultReader`, `Request`, `Response`, `SubtleCrypto`, `TextDecoder`, `TextDecoderStream`, `TextEncoder`, `TextEncoderStream`, `TransformStream`, `TransformStreamDefaultController`, `URL`, `URLSearchParams`, `WebSocket`, `WritableStream`, `WritableStreamDefaultController`, `WritableStreamDefaultWriter`, `atob`, `btoa`, `clearImmediate`, `clearInterval`, `clearTimeout`, `crypto`, `fetch`, `global`, `navigator`, `performance`, `process`, `queueMicrotask`, `setImmediate`, `setInterval`, `setTimeout` and `structuredClone`. The host engine adds no globals of its own: the delivery sees none of the names through which the judge calls it, neither while it loads nor while it answers. Both ViperJS and the host engine leave `import.meta.url` undefined, where plain Node gives a module the `file:` URL it was loaded from. `judge` passes only when both engines answer every case, and the verdict lists the second run as `host`. A delivery that behaves differently when it detects one of the two fails in the other, but both differ from plain Node in the same ways, so a delivery can recognise any judge, for example by `import.meta.url`, and answer correctly only there. `--plain-node`, below, adds plain Node as a third engine. The host engine deletes `Atomics.wait` before the delivery loads, the one way its built-ins can wait without CPU, and charges each call the process's CPU time from `process.cpuUsage`; once a call uses its budget the engine runs no later case, since the work has already failed.

A pass proves that the delivery answered every case in the engines that ran, and no more. The cases are public, so a delivery can answer them from a table: list many cases, including the edge cases a wrong algorithm would get wrong, as the word count spec does for whitespace. A committee whose members each judge with cases of their own, such as cases the spec does not publish or answers compared with a reference on generated inputs, catches a table where one judge would not. ## Plain Node

`judge --sandbox viper --plain-node` also runs every case in plain Node inside a Docker container. It needs Docker: `judge` checks Docker and pulls the image before it runs anything, as `--sandbox docker` does, and refuses with the same refusals. The container starts from the image `--sandbox-image` names, by default the same pinned image as `--sandbox docker`, and takes `--sandbox-memory` and `--sandbox-cpus` as there. With `--sandbox viper` these three options are accepted only together with `--plain-node`, and `judge` refuses `--plain-node` with any sandbox other than `viper`. The container:

- has no network, a read-only root filesystem, no way to gain privileges and the same process cap as `--sandbox docker`, and runs every case as user 65534 with no Linux capabilities, under a launcher that runs as root with only `SETUID`, `SETGID`, `KILL` and `SYS_RESOURCE`, listed in `NODE_CAPABILITIES` in `packages/cli/src/node-judge.ts`;
- runs Docker's init (`--init`) as its first process and the launcher under it, so every process a case leaves behind is reaped once the launcher's sweep kills it, and no zombie holds a slot of the process cap for the cases after it;
- has no writable `/tmp` and no shared memory, and an empty read-only `/dev/mqueue`;
- lets no case create a SysV shared memory segment, message queue or semaphore, or a POSIX message queue, since `NODE_SYSCTLS` in `packages/cli/src/node-judge.ts` sets their limits to 0 in the container's own IPC namespace, so nothing a case makes outlives it there and holds the container's memory;
- sees one read-only mount, `/judge`, that holds the delivery and the judge's runner and nothing else from your machine;
- holds the delivery at `/judge/<random>/<name>.mjs`, in a directory named afresh for each judgement, and loads it with a real `import()` of its `file:` URL, so `import.meta.url`, `import.meta.resolve`, `process`, `console`, timers and every other Node global are what plain Node gives a module.

Each case runs in a fresh Node process, started by a launcher that never loads the delivery and that kills every other process in the container after each case. Before it imports the delivery, the case process reads the case's arguments and a fresh random token from its standard input and keeps its own references to what it needs to answer; it then writes the answer framed by that token. `judge`, outside the container, accepts only an answer framed by the case's token and compares it with the expected answer itself. A case with no framed answer, two framed answers, an answer that is not a string, number, boolean or null, an exception, or no answer in time fails, so a delivery that writes to stdout or stderr, exits early, patches built-ins or prototypes, or reads the runner's files gains no way to pass a case it answers wrongly.

The budget each case may use is `NODE_CASE_MS` in `packages/cli/src/node-judge.ts`, or the spec's `case_ms`. As its last step before it imports the delivery, the case process writes one byte on file descriptor `STARTED_FD` from `packages/cli/src/node-runner.ts` and closes it, so Node's own start is not charged. From then on the launcher reads, every `NODE_SAMPLE_MS`, the container's `usage_usec` in `CPU_STAT` from `packages/cli/src/container-check.ts`, less its own CPU time. The cgroup counts every task however it ended, a child the kernel reaps itself included, and the launcher keeps the highest reading, so the charge never falls. It reads the state of each task of every other process in `/proc` too: an interval in which no task is runnable is time the case waited by its own choice, such as a timer, `Atomics.wait`, a pipe or a stopped process. A throttled or preempted task stays runnable, so the judge's CPU share and load count as neither. An interval in which no task is runnable but one waits on the disk is free up to one budget and charged beyond it: under host disk or memory pressure that wait is the judge's machine, and in the container, whose root is read-only and whose reads stay in the page cache, a case can only keep waiting there by filling its own memory so its pages are read again, which then costs it. The launcher kills a case once both together reach twice the budget, `CONTENTION_FACTOR` in `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts`. Busy SMT siblings and a loaded memory bus make the same work cost more CPU time without showing in the case's own pressure or throttle counts: one module charged 4.4 s on an idle host was charged 6.1 to 6.5 s under 48 busy shells on this host's 32 CPUs, at 1 CPU and at 0.1 alike. So a case that answers rightly after its budget but before twice it is infra when other work kept at least `OTHER_BUSY_LIMIT` of the host's CPUs busy while it ran: the launcher reads `/proc/stat` around the case and subtracts the container's whole CPU time, so a case cannot add to that share, and a wrong answer stays a fail. The host engine does the same with `os.cpus()` less its own process's CPU time, and runs each call's microtasks inside the call, `microtaskMode` `afterEvaluate`, so they use that call's budget. Its backstop, `nodeBackstopMs`, is larger than the boot allowance `NODE_BOOT_MS` at the container's CPU share plus twice the budget plus what a case's own processes can make it wait and its one free budget of disk waiting, at most its CPU time over the share plus its CPU time, so work cannot reach the backstop by stalling itself.

After it starts the module's evaluation, the host engine runs an empty script in the module's context under the same backstop, which drains that context's own microtask queue. On Node 22.12 the promise of a module's evaluation in a context with `microtaskMode` `afterEvaluate` waits in that queue until some script runs there, so without this step every module would end as `no-module`; on later Node the queue is already empty and the step does nothing.

The container's memory is the judge's choice, `--sandbox-memory`, unless the `judge-cases` block pins `node_memory`: a `judge-run` block refuses `--sandbox viper`, and the spec's `heap` bounds only ViperJS. `runPlan` in `packages/cli/src/run-plan.ts` then refuses a `--sandbox-memory` that differs from the pin, and `judgeNode` runs the container under exactly the pin. Left alone, V8 stops a case at a heap limit of its own, which can lie below `--sandbox-memory`, by aborting it, which a case can also do with `process.abort()`. So the launcher starts each case with V8's `--max-old-space-size` set to `NODE_HEAP_FACTOR` times the container's memory, a factor of 1, so the kernel stops the case before V8 can. A larger factor would also delay V8's collections until the container is full, so a case that only makes garbage would be killed.

The launcher reads the `oom` and `oom_kill` counts from the container's `memory.events`, a file the case cannot write, in one read before it starts each case and again after the case ended and its sweep, and sends both readings with the case's answer. `oom` rises only when the container reaches its own memory limit; `oom_kill` rises for every process the kernel kills for memory in the container, also when the host's global out-of-memory killer picks it. The launcher reads them itself, so no new process has to start in a container the work may have filled, and its sweep never runs while a count is read, since one process does both in turn. Under the memory the judge chose, a case that does not pass while `oom_kill` rose between its own two readings is infra, whether it was killed with signal 9, aborted or exited; a case killed with signal 9 whose counts the launcher cannot read is infra too, which the work cannot cause. Under `node_memory`, a case fails when both counts rose while it ran, whatever it answered, and is infra when only `oom_kill` rose, `hostKillPinned` in `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts`, since that kill did not come from the pinned limit. Every count the judge acts on is the difference between the two readings around one case, so a kill in one case is never charged to another. `caseMemory` in `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts` reads only the case's verdict, whether signal 9 ended it and whether the judge stopped it, the two counts' rise, and the pinned and chosen memory.

The launcher also reads `oom_group_kill` where the kernel gives it, and counts it as 0 where it does not. A container whose cgroup sets `memory.oom.group`, as on the CI runner's Docker, has the kernel kill every process of a case together, so the case's own signal 9 does not show which process filled the memory. When that count rose during a case, `caseMemory` in `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts` names the case as having filled the memory, `caseFilledPinned` or `caseFilledChosen`, and never says its own process used it.

A `node_memory` pin is sound only where the launcher can read both counts, which a cgroup v2 host gives each container in `MEMORY_EVENTS` from `packages/cli/src/container-check.ts`. Under a pin `judge` first starts the launcher with no case, `memoryPinHeld` in `packages/cli/src/node-judge.ts`; a launcher that cannot read them, as on a cgroup v1 host, answers `NODE_UNCOUNTED` from `packages/cli/src/node-runner.ts` instead of ready, and one whose probe case could lower its out-of-memory score answers `NODE_UNLOCKED`, and `judge` refuses before any engine runs a case. The run itself refuses the same way. `judge --watch` probes once when it starts, and stops with exit 78 on a host that cannot honour the pin. The probe has a deadline of `--timeout` of its own, which is not charged to the judgement's `--timeout`, and a copy it could not remove is named in a warning on stderr.

`oom_kill` rises for every process the kernel kills for memory, so a case that forks a process the kernel kills for memory and then does not pass is infra under the memory the judge chose, since the judge chose that memory, and passes when it answers rightly. A container that ends before every case was answered, the launcher killed for memory included, is infra, with the reason Docker's state gives. Under `node_memory`, a launcher that ended with Docker's out-of-memory flag or with signal 9 is infra too, `pinnedLauncherEnd` in `packages/cli/src/verdict-model.ts`: the flag names no process and rises for the host's killer too, and no case can signal the launcher or the container's init, so a kill without the flag came from outside the judgement, such as a user-space out-of-memory killer, `docker kill` or an operator, or Docker missed the flag. Unlike a `judge-run` check, which runs the work as its own user, the launcher is out of the work's reach, and the kernel picks a case's process before it, so this infra gives the work no way out of a fail. Under `node_memory` a count the launcher cannot read around a case is infra too. After each case the launcher kills what is left and waits, up to `NODE_SETTLE_MS` in `packages/cli/src/node-judge.ts`, until only the init and itself remain in `MEMORY_PROCS`, before it reads the count after; a case that starts before that and whose count rises is infra under `node_memory`, since the rise may belong to the case before it.

The launcher runs as root and starts each case as root, in a session of its own, since the kernel lets a process send `SIGCONT` to any process in its session. Before the case imports the delivery, it writes `CASE_OOM_SCORE` from `packages/cli/src/node-runner.ts`, 1000, to its own `/proc/self/oom_score_adj` while it still holds `SYS_RESOURCE`, which also sets the lowest score that process and every process it starts may write later, and then drops to user and group 65534 with `process.setgroups`, `process.setgid` and `process.setuid`; a case that cannot do all of this answers `UNPREPARED`, an engine fault, which is infra. A score of 1000 adds the container's whole memory to the score of every process of the case, so the kernel picks one of them before the launcher, both at the container's limit and for the host's global killer, and many small processes cannot make it pick the launcher. The kernel keeps a lower score from a process without `SYS_RESOURCE`, and the dropped case is no longer dumpable, so its own `/proc/self/oom_score_adj` belongs to root. Docker grants that lock only where its capabilities count outside a user namespace, so under `node_memory` the launcher first runs a probe case that has `/bin/sh` try to lower its own score, and answers `NODE_UNLOCKED` unless the score stays at 1000. That change of user clears every capability the launcher holds, and `no-new-privileges` keeps the case from gaining one back through a setuid or file-capability program. The kernel then refuses the case every route to the launcher, the case's parent, whatever the host's Yama `ptrace_scope`: `kill` to the launcher fails with `EPERM`, and its `/proc/<pid>/mem`, `/proc/<pid>/fd/*`, `/proc/<pid>/environ` and a write to its `/proc/<pid>/oom_score_adj` fail with `EACCES`, since reaching another user's process takes a capability the case does not hold. The case holds only its own pipes, so it cannot write into the channel `judge` reads. A launcher that finds itself under the cases' user runs no case, which is infra. Before any case, the launcher writes `KEEPER_OOM_SCORE` from `packages/cli/src/node-runner.ts`, -1000, to its own and to the container's init's `oom_score_adj` and runs no case if either does not hold, so the kernel never picks either of them for a memory kill, even while a killed case's memory is still being freed.

The launcher and every case start with `--disable-sigusr1`, so a case cannot send the launcher `SIGUSR1` to open its debugger on the loopback interface, which `--network none` keeps, and run code in it. An image whose Node does not accept the option, older than Node 22.14, runs no case, which is infra.

The verdict lists this run as `node`, beside `cases` and `host`: `{ "engine": "node-docker", "image", "path", "total", "passed", "failures" }`, where `path` is the delivery's path inside the container. `judge` passes only when every engine it ran answered every case. Without `--plain-node` nothing changes: no Docker is needed and the verdict has no `node`.

`--timeout` bounds the whole judgement, not each engine: the engines share one deadline, and an engine that starts with less than `--timeout` left gets only what is left. An engine the deadline stops is infra, listed in that engine's `stop` with its unfinished cases, unless another engine showed the work fail a case.

A pass still proves behaviour only in the engines that ran. A delivery can detect every judge engine, the plain Node one included, for example by the container's paths, its user, the missing network or the read-only files, and behave differently elsewhere; `--plain-node` closes only the difference between the judge and plain Node itself. ## Payee

`judge` lists the escrow's announcements from accounts and pays the first, in `seq` order, whose sealed salt opens its commitment to the work's hash. It refuses before running anything when the list is incomplete: a seq below the delivery book's next seq is missing, or the escrow is closed. An open escrow without a delivery book has no announcements yet, so its list is complete and empty and the judge says nobody has announced. It refuses when an earlier announcement is sealed to a key it was not given, naming that key; when another worker holds the job's claim; and when no announcement opens to the work. An earlier announcement sealed to a key it holds that does not open can never open for anyone, so it is passed over and listed in the verdict's `skipped`. So is one that is not a delivery: its wallet slot names a key other than the one derived from the wallet the program assigns to it, the judge's or the committee member's at that position, or a committee escrow's announcement is sealed to more or fewer recipients than the committee has members. Each skipped entry carries its `reason` and a plain `why`. The payee is never a free choice: the release pays the announcing worker's token account, and the program checks the opening against the announcement's commitment.

## Hashing work

A file hashes as the SHA-256 of its bytes, so its name does not matter and any sha256 tool gives the same hash. A directory hashes as `manifestSha256` over every file under it, by its path relative to the directory with forward slashes, skipping `.git`, `node_modules` and `target`. `judge` refuses work that holds a symbolic link or any other entry that is neither a file nor a directory. A worker announces this hash, which `intent-engine hash --artifact <file or directory>` prints on every platform; `intent-engine deliver --artifact <file or directory>` announces the same hash without copying it. `judge`, `hash` and `deliver` refuse, before reading the contents of any file, a file larger than 67108864 bytes, a directory whose files add up to more than that, or a directory of more than 10000 files; nothing is run or paid. Work that is one file links to that file itself; work that is several files links to an archive, which the judge unpacks into a directory before it judges it.

## MCP releases

An MCP server started with `--wallet` lists the same announcements with `solive_wallet_check_delivery`, opens one and fetches its work with `solive_wallet_fetch_work`, which returns the first announcement that opens to the fetched sha256 with any it skipped, and releases with `solive_wallet_attest`, which needs `delivered_sha256` for a proof escrow and pays that first announcement, refusing as `judge` does. `solive_wallet_attest_split` pays a split whose recipients include that announcement's worker. Neither runs or fetches anything else, so run the check with `judge` or `solive_wallet_judge_work` first. `solive_wallet_refute` and `solive_wallet_reclaim` wrap `refute` and `reclaim`.

## Receipts

A signature needs a passing run. On every `pass`, and never on `fail` or `infra`, `judge` and `solive_wallet_judge_work` write a receipt with `packages/cli/src/judge-receipt.ts`. It records the cluster's genesis hash, the judge, the escrow's owner, nonce and intent, the spec's sha256, the work's sha256, the first announcement that opens to it, and the route: the sandbox, the command, the image, the `judge-run` block and the ViperJS pins.

The receipt lives at `~/.config/intent-engine/receipts/<sha256>.json`, named by the sha256 of the cluster, judge, intent, announcement and work, so no path holds untrusted text. The directory is created with mode 0700 and each file with 0600, written to a temporary file and renamed into place. An HMAC-SHA256 under a 32-byte key in that directory, made once, seals each receipt.

One gate, `packages/cli/src/receipt-gate.ts`, runs before any preview approval, Ledger prompt or send in `solive_wallet_attest`, `solive_wallet_attest_split`, `solive_wallet_committee_vote` and `committee vote`. It refuses a symbolic link, a file or directory owned by another user or open to other users, a receipt whose mac fails, an outcome other than `pass`, a field that differs from what is signed, and a ViperJS pin this CLI does not hold.

A committee release signs only as fee payer: it relays votes each member's own gate passed, so it needs no receipt. In `solive_wallet_committee_release` a member that signs its own co-author share signs the vote's bytes, and the program counts that signature as a vote, so that one signature needs the member's own receipt; a co-author outside the committee needs none. `committee release` signs no share itself.

`judge` signing its own fresh pass needs no receipt, so `judge --yes` signs as before. An attested escrow without a spec hash has no spec to judge and is not gated. `judge digest` signs nothing.

The override, `--without-receipt <reason>` on `committee vote` or `without_receipt` over MCP, needs a non-empty reason. It is never read from a setting or the environment. The output records it as `unchecked`, with a warning naming the escrow, spec, work and announcement that were not checked; the CLI also prints that warning on stderr. Over MCP the reason changes the preview's `approval`, so a send cannot add or drop it.

The work cannot plant a receipt from a Docker or ViperJS check, which never sees the home directory. A check run with `--sandbox none --i-trust-this-work` runs as you and can write one, as it can read your keypair.

## Committees

`committee release` checks every vote against the escrow before it sends, and the program checks them again: every vote must sign the same escrow, announcement, work, salt and result, and the release needs at least `threshold` members. The judge fee goes to the committee's fee wallet. `committee show --committee <address>` prints its terms. Members who run different checks cover each other's blind spots; members who all run the same check do not.

## Claims

A worker that cannot finish gives the job up with `claims drop` and gets its stake back. Every other worker may take the job at once; the worker that dropped it may take it again only once the claim it dropped would have ended. A worker that passes the job through a second wallet of its own can still renew its hold, as the threat model's T-74 says. Once the escrow is released, cancelled or expired, anyone settles the board with `claims settle --keypair <payer file> --owner --nonce`, which closes it and returns its rent to the owner; while the escrow is open, it only ends a lapsed claim.

Who gets the stake:

- A release that pays the claimant returns its stake to it.
- A worker that drops its claim gets its stake back.
- A claim that lapses forfeits its stake to the owner, when the next worker takes the job, when the release pays someone else, when the owner cancels, or when anyone settles the board.
- A claim that still holds when the owner cancels the escrow is refunded to its worker by the cancel itself, so waiting for the claim to lapse after a cancel gains the owner nothing. A claim never outlives the escrow, so at expiry it has lapsed.
